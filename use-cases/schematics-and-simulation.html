Schematics and Simulation

Electronic and industrial electrical schematics on the same canvas as the 3D part, with real libraries and a built-in simulation.

Use it freeHow it works
The problem

Why this is hard today

The schematic lives in one tool, the bill of materials in a spreadsheet, the enclosure in a CAD viewer and the review in a chat. Industrial diagrams are worse: generic symbols with no terminals and no part data, so nothing can be checked.

How Ensemble helps

From scattered files to one workspace.

01

Pick the kind of schematic

Electronic and industrial electrical are two separate kinds, each its own add-on with its own symbol library. Electronic uses the KiCad libraries with their pins and board footprints. Industrial uses the IEC symbols with their terminals and the ETIM classification of each device.

02

Build it in one panel

Components tab: search the library and place real parts. Properties tab: value, brand, model, footprint or ETIM data, and the terminal marking of the device. Replace a symbol, find alternatives, link the schematic to the sketch it came from.

03

Check it

Simulate tab: write the voltage of each supply and the power or resistance of each load, open and close the contacts, and see the voltage, the current and the power of every part painted on the schematic. Make the bill of materials and export the netlist or an SVG.

What you get

Built into the workspace

The circuit is solved as direct current. Three-phase loads are sized at rated operation from their power, cos φ and efficiency, not as a full AC network, and cables are sized for copper in PVC. It is a quick check of ideas, not a replacement for a dedicated electrical design tool.

  • Electronic: about 23,000 KiCad symbols with real pins, and KiCad board footprints you can pick, preview and add to the bill of materials
  • Industrial: about 6,400 IEC/EN 60617 symbols with their terminals, and ETIM 10.0 classes and technical data for each device
  • Terminal marking according to IEC 60947 from the poles that ETIM gives
  • Simulation of both kinds in the Simulate tab: voltage drop and power, with editable supplies, loads and ratings, and contacts you can open
  • Control logic: buttons, coils and the contacts they pull in, including a start-stop circuit that seals itself in
  • Cables, protections, phases and cabinet heat checked from the same schematic
  • Warnings: a load above what a resistor handles, a fuse that carries more than its rating, a cable too thin for its breaker, a supply that is missing
  • A bill of materials table straight from the schematic, with the footprint of each part
  • The language of the app follows your device, and the libraries keep their own names
